An AI companion for seniors is a product that talks with an older person, usually by voice, and often adds simple extras like reminders. It can make a quiet day a little less quiet and give a parent something to talk to between your calls. It is not a doctor, not an emergency service, and not a replacement for the people in your parent's life.

This guide explains what these products do well, where they stop, and the questions worth asking before you put one in front of your mom or dad.

## What an AI companion is, in plain words

Think of it as a conversation partner that never gets tired and is always available. Your parent speaks, it answers in a friendly voice, and it can bring up topics like the weather, old hobbies, music or a favorite memory. Some products run on a phone, some on a tablet, and some are a small tabletop robot.

Two things are worth knowing from the start:

- **It is software, not a person.** A good one tells your parent it is an AI. If a product hides that, treat it as a red flag.
- **The category is wide.** Some companions only chat. Others add reminders, games, photo sharing, or a way for family to be involved. Always check what a specific product actually includes.

## What AI companions can do

### Keep a parent company between calls

Many older adults spend long stretches of the day without anyone to talk to. A companion can fill small gaps: a chat over morning coffee, a quiz while the news is on, a place to say what's on their mind. This is where most families see value.

### Handle small routines

Depending on the product, a companion may offer medication or appointment reminders, music, trivia, or gentle suggestions to get up and move. ElliQ, a tabletop companion robot, lists medication reminders, exercise suggestions, music, news, trivia and games on its official site. Phone apps vary much more, so read the listing.

### Give you something to talk about

When you call and ask "What did you do today?", the answer is often "Nothing much." If your parent has been chatting with a companion about a recipe or a favorite singer, you have a new thread to pull on.

### Let family stay in the loop, if the product supports it

Some products include a family side, such as photo sharing, video calls, or a view of what your parent chooses to share. Ask whether your parent controls what you see. That control matters for dignity and trust.

## What AI companions can't do

Being honest about the limits protects your parent.

- **They can't replace human contact.** ElliQ's own caregiver page describes its robot as "a helping hand, not a replacement" for human care. Good products say this themselves.
- **They are not medical devices.** ElliQ states that it is not a medical device. The EverFriends app listing on the App Store says its companions are not a substitute for professional medical care, therapists, or emergency services. Questions about symptoms or medicines belong with a doctor or pharmacist.
- **Most can't call for help.** Unless a product clearly says it connects to emergency services, assume it does not. ElliQ's page says that for emergencies, members should contact their caregiver and trusted contacts.
- **They can get things wrong.** AI can say something confident and incorrect. Remind your parent not to act on health, money or legal advice from a chatbot.
- **They can't see your parent.** A phone app or a talking device does not know if your parent has fallen unless the product has special hardware, and most do not.
- **Some parents will not like them.** That is fine. Forcing a companion on a parent who finds it silly tends to backfire.

## Safety and privacy questions to ask before you choose

These are questions to put to the maker, read in the privacy policy, or check in the App Store listing.

1. **Does it say clearly that it is an AI?** Your parent should never be unsure whether a person is on the other end.
2. **What is saved?** Is the audio recorded and kept, or only a short summary of the conversation? Can your parent see and delete what is saved?
3. **Who sees the conversations?** Do family members see everything, only what your parent shares, or nothing?
4. **Is the data sold or used for ads?** Look for a plain answer, not a vague one. The App Store privacy section on an app's page lists what data it collects. It is self-reported, and Apple notes that it has not verified it, so it is a starting point, not a guarantee.
5. **How does billing work?** Check whether it is a one-time fee, a monthly plan, or minutes that run out. Confirm how to cancel before you sign up, and whether your parent can accidentally buy something.
6. **What happens in a crisis?** If your parent says they feel hopeless or unwell, does the product point them to a person or a helpline, or just keep chatting?
7. **How hard is it to use?** Try it yourself first. If you get lost in the first five minutes, your parent will too.
8. **Who is the company?** Look for a real address, a support contact, and a privacy policy written in plain language.

> If a product promises to cure loneliness, prevent falls or replace caregivers, walk away. A trustworthy companion is a supplement to people, not a substitute.

## Robot or phone app?

Both can work. Neither is better in every home.

| | Tabletop robot | Phone app |
|---|---|---|
| Hardware | A dedicated device that stays in one room | Uses a phone your parent may already own |
| Setup | Often plugged in and connected to Wi-Fi at home | Download, sign in, grant microphone permission |
| Presence | Always visible, can start conversations on its own | Your parent opens the app, or responds to a reminder |
| Cost model | Often a membership or lease; check current terms | Often free to download with a paid plan; check the listing |
| Portability | Stays home | Goes wherever the phone goes |
| Good fit | A parent who is home most of the day and likes a device that is simply there | A parent who already uses a smartphone comfortably |

If your parent doesn't use a smartphone confidently, a dedicated device may be less stressful. If they already text and take photos, an app may feel natural. For a side-by-side look at one robot and the phone-app route, see our [ElliQ alternatives guide](/en/guide/elliq-alternatives).

## How families can stay involved

A companion works best as one piece of a wider routine.

- **Introduce it together.** Sit with your parent for the first conversation. Let them decide whether they like it.
- **Keep the phone calls.** The companion should add contact, not subtract it.
- **Ask what they talked about.** "Did you chat with it today?" is a gentle way to hear how they are doing.
- **Agree on what is shared.** Decide together what family can see, and respect it.
- **Have a human backup.** Keep a neighbor, friend or local service on your contact list. In the US, the [Eldercare Locator](https://eldercare.acl.gov) (1-800-677-1116) connects families to local services. In the UK, the Age UK Advice Line is 0800 678 1602, free to call from 8am to 7pm every day of the year.
- **Review after a few weeks.** If it isn't helping, stop. There is no prize for sticking with it.

For more ideas on keeping in touch without hovering, read [how to check in on an elderly parent who lives alone](/en/guide/check-in-on-elderly-parent-living-alone).

## FAQ

### Is an AI companion good for an elderly parent who is lonely?

It can help fill quiet moments with conversation, but it works best alongside people, not instead of them. Keep up your calls and visits, and encourage contact with neighbors, friends and community groups. If your parent seems persistently sad or withdrawn, please ask their doctor.

### Are AI companions safe for seniors?

Safety depends on the specific product. Choose one that clearly says it is an AI, explains what it saves, lets your parent delete their data, and is open about pricing. No AI companion is a medical or emergency device, so keep a human plan for emergencies.

### What is the difference between an AI companion robot and an AI companion app?

A robot is a dedicated device that sits at home, usually plugged in and connected to Wi-Fi. An app runs on a phone your parent already has. The robot is always visible in the room; the app travels with the phone. Which fits better depends on how comfortable your parent is with a smartphone.
