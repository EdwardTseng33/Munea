Caring for aging parents from a distance works best when you set up a few simple systems before a crisis: people nearby you can call, a regular rhythm of contact, one up-to-date set of records, and visits that are planned rather than rushed. You can't be there every day, and you don't need to be. This checklist covers ten things to put in place, most of which take an afternoon each. It is written for the adult children who are caregivers, or carers as many in the UK and Australia say, for a parent who lives far away.

## 1. Ask your parent what they want

Start with a conversation, not a plan. Ask what they want help with, what they want to keep doing themselves, and what they would rather you stay out of. The rest of this list goes better when your parent is part of it. Write down what you agree on.

## 2. Build a contact list and put it in more than one place

Gather these in one note, and give a copy to a sibling or trusted relative:

- your parent's doctor, pharmacy and dentist
- two neighbors or friends nearby, with their phone numbers
- family members who can step in
- the building manager, landlord or home maintenance person
- your own number, plus a backup contact for you

Ask each local person whether they are happy to be listed. Tell them how to reach you.

## 3. Find your local help network

Find out what exists near your parent before you need it.

- **United States:** the [Eldercare Locator](https://eldercare.acl.gov) is a public service of the Administration for Community Living. Call or text 1-800-677-1116, or email, to find services, including Area Agencies on Aging and community services such as home-delivered meals.
- **United Kingdom:** [Age UK](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/information-advice/care/arranging-care/) runs a free Advice Line on 0800 678 1602, open 8am to 7pm, 365 days a year, and says it has specialist advisers at over 120 local Age UKs. Contacting the local council's social services department is the first step to a care needs assessment, according to the same page.
- **Canada and Australia:** start with your provincial or national aging services, or ask your parent's doctor or local council where to begin. In Australia, Carers Australia lists the Carer Gateway phone line on 1800 422 737 for carers.

## 4. Agree a call rhythm that you can keep

A short call at a set time beats a long call you skip. Pick days and times with your parent, agree when you will try again if they don't pick up, and decide who you will call next. If you have siblings, take turns so one person is not always the one on the phone. For more on routines and questions to ask, see [How to check in on an elderly parent living alone](/en/guide/check-in-on-elderly-parent-living-alone).

## 5. Keep one medication list, with their doctor or pharmacist

MedlinePlus Magazine, published by the National Library of Medicine, suggests keeping a medication list with dosing times, plus a regularly updated list of doctors and other contacts. Ask your parent's doctor or pharmacist to confirm that the list is right, and ask your parent for permission before you speak with their medical team. Questions about what to take belong with the doctor or pharmacist, not with you. If you want to explore reminders, read [medication reminder apps for seniors: what to look for](/en/guide/medication-reminder-apps-for-seniors).

## 6. Get written permission to talk with doctors

Ask your parent to sign the forms their doctor's office uses so you can talk with the care team. MedlinePlus Magazine says to get written permission to speak with the medical team so you can stay informed. Offices have their own forms, so ask the front desk what they require.

## 7. Plan visits so they count

Before each visit, ask your parent what they would like to do, whether it is grocery shopping, seeing friends or a doctor's appointment. With their permission, schedule appointments during your stay. Then use part of the visit for a quick, friendly look around the house:

- Are the stairs, hallways and bathroom safe to move around in?
- Is the lighting good? Are there loose rugs or clutter?
- Is the fridge stocked with food that is in date?
- Are the smoke alarms working?
- Is the mail piling up?

Keep it light. You are checking the home, not inspecting your parent. And set aside time to simply enjoy each other, such as a shared hobby.

## 8. Organize the legal and financial papers, with a professional

Find out where the important papers are and who has copies. MedlinePlus Magazine recommends keeping legal, financial and medical records current and easy to find. The documents differ by country, so ask a lawyer, solicitor or financial adviser who works with older clients what your parent needs. Two examples:

- **United States:** MedlinePlus describes advance directives, such as a living will and a durable power of attorney for health care, which names a health care proxy.
- **England and Wales:** [Age UK](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/information-advice/money-legal/legal-issues/power-of-attorney/) explains that a lasting power of attorney lets someone you trust make decisions if you lose mental capacity, in two types (property and financial affairs, and health and care), and it must be registered before use. The page says you don't have to use a solicitor, but it could prevent problems later.
- **Canada:** if your parent depends on you because of an infirmity, ask a tax professional about the [Canada caregiver credit](https://www.canada.ca/caregiver-credit), which the CRA says can apply to a parent who depends on you regularly and consistently for basics (your parent must have lived in Canada at some point in the year). Keep your records.

Raise these topics gently and early, while your parent can lead the decisions.

## 9. Spread the load and invite local help in

If siblings or relatives are involved, agree who handles what: calls, bills, appointments, visits. MedlinePlus Magazine recommends building a local care network of nearby family, friends or neighbors who can help, especially in emergencies, and considering local caregivers or care managers to coordinate care. Time your visits so the person who does the most day to day can take a break.

## 10. Look after yourself

Distance doesn't make this lighter. It often adds guilt. Decide what you can realistically do, say no to the rest, and keep up your own doctor visits, sleep and friendships. If you are exhausted, ask for respite, which means getting someone else to cover for a while. In the US, the Eldercare Locator can point you to local services, and in the UK, Age UK's advisers can talk through options. In Australia, the Carer Gateway line is 1800 422 737.

## Where Munea fits, and what it doesn't do

Munea is an iPhone app (iOS 15 or later) in which your parent can chat by voice with an AI character, Nina or Milo, and get medication and appointment reminders. Family members in your parent’s family circle can see their health and mood updates, and every member of a circle needs a Plus or Pro plan right now. It has no fall detection, emergency button, location sharing, cameras or sensors, and it won't call your parent or alert you if the app isn't opened. It is on the App Store in the US and Canada, and not currently available in the UK or Australia.

## FAQ

### How do I take care of elderly parents who live far away?

Set up a regular call, a few local contacts, one shared set of records and planned visits. Learn what services exist near your parent, and ask your parent what help they want. Add up what you can do, share it with siblings, and look after yourself.

### What is a long-distance caregiver?

It is someone who helps a parent or relative who lives far away, with tasks such as managing bills, arranging care, keeping in touch and handling emergencies. There is no official distance; what matters is that you can't pop over when something comes up.

### How often should I visit an aging parent who lives far away?

There is no right number. Many people plan visits around appointments, holidays and times when a sibling or neighbor needs a break. Ask your parent what they would like, and use calls and video chats in between.
