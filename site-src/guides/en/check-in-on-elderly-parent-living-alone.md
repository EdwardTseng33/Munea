The best way to check in on an elderly parent who lives alone is a predictable routine that both of you agree to: a call at a set time, a few specific questions, a local person you can reach, and clear rules about what you will and won't ask. Tools can help, but the relationship does the heavy lifting. The aim is for your parent to feel looked out for, not watched.

This guide is for adult children, whether you call yourself a caregiver or, as many in the UK and Australia say, a carer, who live far away and want to stay close without hovering.

## Start with a conversation, not a system

Before you buy anything or set up a schedule, ask your parent what would feel comfortable. Many older adults worry about becoming a burden or losing independence. If a plan arrives as a decision made about them, they may quietly resist it.

Try wording like this:

- "I think about you a lot. What would make it easy for us to stay in touch?"
- "Would you rather I call every day, or a few times a week?"
- "Is there anything you'd like me to stay out of?"

Write down what you agree on. A plan your parent helped shape is one they are more likely to keep.

## Build a simple routine

Regularity matters more than length. A five-minute call at the same time each day or every other day is easier to keep up, and it makes a missed call stand out.

1. **Pick a time.** Choose one that fits their day, such as after breakfast or before the evening news.
2. **Pick a backup.** If they don't answer, agree in advance when you will try again, and who you will try next (a neighbor, a friend, a relative nearby).
3. **Vary the channel.** Some days a video call, some days a plain phone call, some days a text or photo exchange.
4. **Keep a shared note.** A paper notebook near the phone or a shared note for siblings helps everyone know what was said.
5. **Share the load.** If you have siblings or relatives, split the days. One person calling every day tends to burn out.

## What to ask on your calls

"How are you?" usually gets "Fine." Specific, friendly questions get more truthful answers.

- "What did you have for lunch?" (Tells you about appetite and energy without sounding like a quiz.)
- "Did you get outside today?"
- "Who have you talked to this week?"
- "Is anything around the house giving you trouble? A light, a step, the heating?"
- "How did you sleep?"
- "Do you need anything from the store or pharmacy?"
- "What's the best thing that happened today?"

Pay attention to changes more than single answers. If you notice repeated confusion, missed meals, a messy house that used to be tidy, or a change in mood that lasts, mention it to their doctor with your parent's agreement. Please leave questions about medicines and symptoms to their doctor or pharmacist.

## Line up local help before you need it

When you live far away, a few local contacts make a big difference.

- **Neighbors and friends.** Ask one or two if they would be willing to be a contact and exchange phone numbers. Be clear it is a favor, and offer to return it in some way.
- **A key plan.** Agree who can enter the home if you can't reach your parent, and where the key is kept.
- **Local services.** In the US, the [Eldercare Locator](https://eldercare.acl.gov) is a public service of the Administration for Community Living. You can call or text 1-800-677-1116, or search online, to find local services, including those provided through Area Agencies on Aging. In the UK, [Age UK](https://www.ageuk.org.uk/information-advice/care/arranging-care/) runs a free Advice Line on 0800 678 1602, open 8am to 7pm every day of the year, and its website says it has specialist advisers at over 120 local Age UKs. Age UK also offers a free weekly friendship call, in which a volunteer is matched with the person; you can ask about it through the Age UK website or Advice Line.
- **Doctor and pharmacy.** With your parent's permission, keep the contact details in your phone.

If you live in Canada or Australia, your provincial or national aging services can serve the same purpose. Ask your parent's doctor or local council where to start.

## Types of apps and tools, described fairly

No app can do what a regular call and a neighbor can. Still, tools can make the routine easier. They fall into a few groups, and each trades something for something else.

| Type | What it generally does | What to think about |
|---|---|---|
| Check-in apps | Your parent taps a button or answers a prompt each day, and family is notified if they don't | Needs a regular habit; can feel like a daily test |
| Location sharing | Lets chosen people see where a phone is | Shows where, not how they are; privacy is the main question |
| Companion apps and devices | Your parent chats, gets reminders, and may share updates | Adds company and routine; check what family sees and what it can't do |
| Video-call devices | Simplify calls for parents who find phones hard | Good for connection; no daily prompt unless you set one |

Whichever you choose, ask these questions:

1. Did your parent say yes?
2. What exactly will family see, and can your parent change that?
3. What happens if the app is not opened? (Many apps do nothing.)
4. Does it need a smartphone, Wi-Fi, a subscription?
5. Is it described plainly as not being a medical or emergency service?

## When a tool is not enough

Apps and chats are for ordinary days. If your parent is at risk of falling, has a health condition that can change quickly, or you need to know right away if something happens, talk with their doctor about a medical alert device or in-person care. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

## FAQ

### How often should I check in on my parent who lives alone?

There is no single right number. Many families settle on a short daily call or a few calls a week, plus a text or photo in between. Ask your parent what feels comfortable, then stick to the pattern so that a missed call is noticeable.

### Is there an app to check on elderly parents?

Yes, several kinds exist: daily check-in apps, location sharing, and companion apps that chat and send reminders. They do different jobs, so decide what you need first. None replaces a regular call, a nearby contact and a plan for emergencies.

### What should I do if my parent doesn't answer the phone?

Try again after the time you agreed on, then call the backup person you chose in advance, such as a neighbor or relative nearby. If you are worried and cannot reach anyone, ask someone to visit, or call your local emergency number if you believe there is an emergency.
